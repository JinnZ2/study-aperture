# Worked example — findings: Carney, Cuddy & Yap (2010), "Power posing"

This is the narrative record behind `assessment.json` / `report.md`. It was produced with the study-aperture workflow: route, extract claims, declare premises, route agreement, keep alternatives alive, label records. It is an assessment record, not a verdict on anyone. It was **not** produced blind: the assessor knew the controversy in advance, and the power-pose literature is one of the most publicly litigated cases in psychology — treat it accordingly.

**Materials actually held:** the original article's journal full text was consulted to verify every reported statistic quoted below; the replication, p-curve, and commentary literature was verified through excerpts of the primary texts obtained via search; the first author's 2016 statement was read via excerpts. Where a design detail (e.g., whether a specific replication inherited a specific procedure) was not verifiable from the excerpts in hand, it is marked as not established rather than inferred.

## The publication

Carney, Cuddy & Yap (2010), [Psychological Science 21(10):1363–1368](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610383437). Verified against the journal full text: high-power posers reported feeling more "powerful" and "in charge" (M=2.57 vs 1.83, F(1,41)=9.53, p<.01, r=.44); took the gambling risk more often (χ²(1, N=42)=3.86, p<.05, Φ=.30); showed a treatment effect on testosterone (F(1,39)=4.29, p<.05, r=.34); and showed a cortisol decrease where low-power posers showed an increase (F(1,38)=7.45, p<.02, r=.43) ([journal full text](https://journals.sagepub.com/stoken/rbtfl/4zEmVxPysrEHI/full)). N=42 is the reported analysis sample; the article text consulted does not mention exclusions.

## Claim-by-claim outcome

**C1 — felt power: PARTIALLY_SUPPORTED.** Ranehill et al. (2015) replicated the felt-power increase with N=200 and 95% power, while finding no hormone or behavioral effects ([FORRT replication record](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1177/0956797610383437/)). A Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis of preregistered studies found a small positive effect on felt power ([Gronau et al. 2017, discussed here](https://www.clearerthinking.org/post/shifting-your-stance-on-the-psychological-impacts-of-power-posing-from-real-to-fake-to-small)). But Ronay et al. (2016), an exact replication of the original methodology, found no support for the felt-power effect ([Comprehensive Results in Social Psychology](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23743603.2016.1248081)), and Garrison et al. (2016), preregistered, found the expansive pose *reduced* feelings of power ([SPPS](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/Social-Psychological-and-Personality-Science-2016-Garrison-1948550616652209-copy.pdf)).

**C2 — risk tolerance: UNSUPPORTED.** No effect in Ranehill et al. (2015) (title: "No effect on hormones and risk tolerance"), Ronay et al. (2016), or Garrison et al. (2016) (gambling decision).

**C3 testosterone / C4 cortisol: UNSUPPORTED.** Null in Ranehill et al. (2015) (hormones measured, no effect) and Ronay et al. (2016) (T2 testosterone F(1,102)=0.39, p=0.53; cortisol F(1,100)=1.40, p=0.24); whether Garrison et al. (2016) measured hormones is not established from the excerpts in hand, so it is not counted here either way. A multiverse re-analysis found the original hormone inferences highly sensitive to data-analytic specifications ([Revisiting the Power Pose Effect](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/TITLE_Revisiting_the_Power_Pose_Effect_H.pdf)); and Simmons & Simonsohn's p-curve of the 33 studies cited in the authors' own replication response concluded the distribution of p-values was indistinguishable from selective reporting around a zero effect ([p-curve paper](https://repository.upenn.edu/entities/publication/203987d6-6558-47fb-882d-7b89a4a96fe7)).

## What the cards caught

**Card 02 (register map) — the highest-value catches.** The first author's own 2016 statement discloses: 47 participants run, 5 excluded on bases like "didn't follow directions" (exclusions not mentioned in the article; disclosed only in 2016 → `MEASURED_DISCARDED` without a discard rule); data collection in chunks (25, then 10, then 7, then 5) with effects checked along the way (no stopping rule); and the self-report measure p-hacked — "many different power questions were asked and those chosen were the ones that 'worked'" ([Carney, "My position on power poses"](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/dana_carney/pdf_my%20position%20on%20power%20poses.pdf), [The Cut summary](https://www.thecut.com/2016/09/power-poses-co-author-i-dont-think-power-poses-are-real.html)). She also identifies the testosterone layer's instrument contamination: participants learned whether they had won the gamble before saliva sampling, and winning alone can raise testosterone — "this testosterone effect... may merely be a winning effect" ([Chronicle](https://www.chronicle.com/article/power-poser/)).

**Card 01 (conditionals) — the retelling, formalized.** An assessor-authored formalization (the retellings are formalized by the assessor, not quoted verbatim from a single text): the original claim formalized as `IF [participants adopt expansive poses] THEN ([felt power rises] AND [risk tolerance rises] AND [testosterone rises] AND [cortisol falls])`. Three comparisons:

- Antecedent loss, shown directly: comparing `IF [participants adopt expansive poses] THEN [risk tolerance rises]` against the bare `[risk tolerance rises]` returns `ASSERTED_CONSEQUENT` — "operator dropped: premise deleted, consequent stated as unconditional fact." That is the named collapse the retelling commits.
- Retelling A, the single conjunct `[felt power rises]`: `NOT_ENTAILED`. The restatement is not stronger or weaker than the original — with the antecedent dropped, the two sentences are **logically incomparable** (neither entails the other). What is lost is the conditional structure, which is exactly what the 2010 abstract's "confirmed our prediction" phrasing rests on.
- Retelling B, the TED-style `IF [participants adopt expansive poses] THEN [life outcomes improve significantly]`: `NOT_ENTAILED`, with a new atom ([Slate's contemporaneous account](https://slate.com/technology/2016/01/amy-cuddys-power-pose-research-is-the-latest-example-of-scientific-overreach.html) quotes the talk's claim that power poses "can significantly change the outcomes of your life") that no original conjunct covers.

The reading is deliberately boring: the retellings are neither entailed by nor entail the four-outcome original — they are different sentences, and the tool's job is only to make that visible, not to grade the retelling's plausibility. (Precision note for the simpler antecedent-loss comparison: as material propositions, the bare consequent B *does* entail A→B; the tool's `ASSERTED_CONSEQUENT` verdict is about restatement fidelity — the operator was dropped, the consequent is now stated as an unconditional fact — not about material entailment.)

**Card 01 (claim scope) — narrowing, recorded as narrowing.** The defended claim family moved from "neuroendocrine and behavioral changes" (2010) to "feelings, emotions, affect" (2018 reply) to "postural feedback". Recorded as `REFERENT_NARROWED`: an honest narrowing **if labeled as such** — the 2018 p-curve reply ([Cuddy, Schultz & Fosse 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797617746749)) defends a *different, narrower* claim family than the 2010 paper made. It is illicit drift only where the 2010 hormone/risk claims are still cited as supported; how often that occurs in current citations was not verified by this assessment.

**Card 03 (false_agree) — a field mismatch requiring review, not a verdict.** Routing the agree-set on "expansive postures raise feelings of power": Ranehill2015 vs the Cuddy2018-pcurve corpus returns `FALSE_AGREE_DEFINITION` — the definitions recorded for the two routes differ (direct replication: expansive-vs-contractive on felt power; p-curve corpus: feelings including emotions/affect, mostly without a neutral-pose control, per [the commentary on Cuddy et al. 2018](https://open.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/1723)). The assessor judges the control-structure difference load-bearing for the expansive-vs-neutral question; the tool's route is where to look, not the finding itself.

**Card 03 (safe_provenance) — two graphs, two answers, both labeled honestly.** On **data** lineage (each study derives from its own data collection): all pairs `NO_SHARED_ANCESTOR_IN_DECLARED_GRAPH` — a property of the declared graph, not real-world independence. On **methods** lineage (all three replications derive from Carney2010 methods): `SOME_PAIRS_SHARE_ANCESTORS`, witness "Carney et al. 2010 (methods)". What the shared node contributes is study-specific and was checked per study: Ronay et al. (2016) states its methods were "an exact replication of the methodology described in Carney et al." ([paper](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23743603.2016.1248081)) — so that study carries whatever the shared methodology contributes, including its design features; for Ranehill et al. (2015) and Garrison et al. (2016), inheritance of specific design features is **not established from the excerpts in hand**. Files: `provenance-data.json`, `provenance-methods.json`.

**Card 04 (branch set) — what remains open.** Candidate explanations for the small felt-power effect: (a) expansive pose raises it; (b) contractive pose *lowers* it (difference is harm-driven); (c) demand/expectation effect; (d) publication bias artifact. (d) is partially eliminated by the preregistered-study meta-analysis finding a small effect. **(a) vs (b): NOT ASSESSED by this assessment.** The commentary authors argue the few control-group designs favor (b), citing Ceunen et al. (2014) (expansive pose reduced felt power vs control) and Garrison et al. (2016) (expansive pose reduced felt power) ([commentary](https://open.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/1723)); but the exact pose/control contrasts in those studies were not verified from the primary texts in hand, so the discrimination between (a) and (b) remains open and the commentary's preference is recorded as *their inference*, not an independently verified finding here. Branches (a), (b), (c) remain open; no elimination was completed by this assessment.

## Decision surfaces

- **D1 — rely on power posing as a confidence intervention? UNRESOLVED.** The materials genuinely do not settle it: a small felt-power effect may exist (meta-analysis of preregistered studies), but its direction relative to a neutral posture is unassessed (see Card 04: the (a)/(b) discrimination is open), and one preregistered design reported the expansive pose reduced felt power ([Garrison et al. 2016](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/Social-Psychological-and-Personality-Science-2016-Garrison-1948550616652209-copy.pdf)). Conditional implications, not recommendations: if the use requires a demonstrated positive effect on feelings relative to a neutral baseline, the current record does not provide it; if the use case only tolerates uncertainty about direction and size, that is a different decision requiring different evidence. Whether trying it is safe is **also not established** by this record — absence of demonstrated benefit is not evidence of absence of harm.
- **D2 — treat the hormone/risk claims as established? RESOLVED (assessor): No.** UNSUPPORTED by direct replications at adequate power and by p-curve analysis; the first author disavows the embodied-effects claim ([her statement](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/dana_carney/pdf_my%20position%20on%20power%20poses.pdf)).
- **D3 — spend resources searching for moderators? DEFERRED.** Simmons & Simonsohn: the existing evidence is too fragile to justify moderator search or advocacy ([p-curve paper](https://repository.upenn.edu/entities/publication/203987d6-6558-47fb-882d-7b89a4a96fe7)).

## Incompleteness (the headline)

- D1 is UNRESOLVED, by design: the record separates confidence in claims (graded above) from whether claims justify action (the reader's call).
- The felt-power branch set is open on (a)/(b)/(c); the record does not force a winner.
- Whether Garrison et al. (2016) measured hormones was not verifiable from the material in hand; it is marked not-established rather than inferred either way.

## Tool-behavior lessons recorded during the run

1. **Generic lineage strings misroute.** When two independent replications were both recorded with lineage "independent data collection", `false_agree.py` routed them `SHARED_UPSTREAM` — the tool routes by the fields you record, and identical strings mean identical upstream. Lineage must name the specific data source.
2. **The raw provenance tool scores unknown nodes as independent.** `provenance_graph.py` returns `INDEPENDENT` for nodes not in the declared graph; the scoped adapter `safe_provenance.py` refuses them instead. Prefer the adapter.
3. **Independence is relative to what the edges declare.** Methods lineage and data lineage give different answers on the same literature; both are true and they answer different questions. Say which graph you mean.
